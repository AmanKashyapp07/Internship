# AI Vocabulary — Part 1: Foundations
### How LLMs work and are built

This isn't a technical deep-dive — it's shared vocabulary. Enough to follow a conversation, read a paper's abstract, or not blank when a teammate says "we fine-tuned it" vs "we RAG'd it."

---

## The Big Picture: What Even Is This Thing

### 1. Large Language Model (LLM)
- **Intuition:** A giant pattern-matching machine trained on huge amounts of text to predict "what word comes next" — and that simple skill, at massive scale, produces something that can reason, write, code, and converse.
- **Where it fits:** Everything else in this document is either *how you build one* (tokenization → vectorization → attention → transformer) or *how you use/extend one* (fine-tuning, prompting, RAG, agents).
- **Example:** GPT, Claude, Gemini, Llama — all LLMs, differing in training data, architecture tweaks, and scale.
- **Common confusion:** People say "AI" when they mean "LLM." LLMs are one type of AI model — specifically for language. Image generators, recommendation systems, etc. are AI but not LLMs.

---

## The Data Pipeline: Tokenization → Vectorization → Attention

### 2. Tokenization
- **Intuition:** Before a model can process text, it has to chop it into small chunks — "tokens" — which might be whole words, parts of words, or even single characters, depending on the tokenizer.
- **Where it fits:** This is step zero. Nothing else works until text becomes tokens. It's why LLMs are bad at things like counting letters in a word — they often don't "see" individual letters, they see token chunks.
- **Example:** The word "unbelievable" might tokenize into "un" + "believ" + "able" rather than staying as one unit.
- **Common confusion:** Tokens are not the same as words. This is also why LLM pricing/limits are quoted in "tokens," not words — and why token count often surprises people.

### 3. Vectorization
- **Intuition:** Once you have tokens, you convert each one into a list of numbers (a vector) that captures its *meaning* — so "king" and "queen" end up numerically close, while "king" and "banana" don't.
- **Where it fits:** This turns language into math. It's the bridge between tokenization and everything the neural network actually computes with (attention, transformers).
- **Example:** Word embeddings placing "Paris - France + Italy ≈ Rome" — a famous illustration that vectors capture relationships, not just identity.
- **Common confusion:** Vectorization here (turning text into meaning-carrying numbers) is different from "vectorizing code" in programming (turning loops into batch array operations) — same word, unrelated concept.

### 4. Attention
- **Intuition:** A mechanism that lets the model decide, for each word, which *other* words in the sentence matter most to understanding it — instead of treating every word as equally relevant.
- **Where it fits:** This is the core innovation that made modern LLMs possible. It sits inside the Transformer architecture (see below) and is why models handle long-range context so much better than older approaches.
- **Example:** In "The trophy didn't fit in the suitcase because it was too big," attention helps the model figure out "it" refers to the trophy, not the suitcase — by weighting relevant words more heavily.
- **Common confusion:** "Attention" as used here is a precise mathematical mechanism (weighted relevance scores), not just a metaphor for "the model is paying attention" in the human sense.

## How Models Learn: Self-Supervised Learning → Transformer

### 5. Self-Supervised Learning
- **Intuition:** The training trick that lets LLMs learn from raw, unlabeled internet text — by hiding a word and having the model guess it, the text labels itself.
- **Where it fits:** This is *how* an LLM gets its initial broad knowledge, before any fine-tuning happens. It's what makes training on the entire internet feasible — no human needs to label anything.
- **Example:** Given "The cat sat on the ___," the model predicts "mat" and gets corrected against the real next word — repeated billions of times across the web.
- **Common confusion:** Not the same as "unsupervised learning" (finding patterns with no target at all) or "supervised learning" (needs human-labeled data). Self-supervised sits in between — it invents its own labels from the data itself.

### 6. Transformer
- **Intuition:** The specific neural network architecture — built around the attention mechanism — that almost every modern LLM is based on.
- **Where it fits:** This is the "body" that houses attention and gets trained via self-supervised learning on tokenized, vectorized text. Tokenization + vectorization + attention + self-supervised learning all combine inside a Transformer.
- **Example:** The 2017 paper "Attention Is All You Need" introduced this architecture — it's the reason the "T" in GPT stands for Transformer.
- **Common confusion:** "Transformer" (the architecture) vs "GPT" or "Claude" (specific trained models built using that architecture) — the architecture is the blueprint, the model is the finished building.

## Getting a Model to Do What You Want: Fine-tuning → Few-shot Prompting

### 7. Fine-tuning
- **Intuition:** Taking an already-trained LLM and training it further on a smaller, specific dataset so it gets better at a particular task or domain.
- **Where it fits:** This happens *after* the big self-supervised pretraining — it's specialization, not building from scratch.
- **Example:** Taking a general LLM and fine-tuning it on legal documents so it's sharper at legal-specific tasks and terminology.
- **Common confusion:** Fine-tuning vs RAG (see Part 2) — fine-tuning changes the model's internal weights permanently; RAG just feeds it relevant info at query time without changing the model at all. People often reach for fine-tuning when RAG would be cheaper and more flexible.

### 8. Few-shot Prompting
- **Intuition:** Giving the model a few examples of the task directly in your prompt, so it "picks up the pattern" without any retraining at all.
- **Where it fits:** This is the lightest-weight way to steer a model — no fine-tuning, no extra data pipeline, just smart prompt writing.
- **Example:** "Translate to French: sea otter → loutre de mer, cheese → fromage, hello →" — the model infers it should output "bonjour" from the pattern.
- **Common confusion:** Few-shot vs fine-tuning — few-shot is temporary (only affects that one conversation/prompt), fine-tuning is permanent (bakes the behavior into the model itself).
